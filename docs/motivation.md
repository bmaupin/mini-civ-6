# Motivation

## Overview

Initially I simply wanted a way to play matches of Civ 6 more quickly. But as I began modding, I found that a lot of the systems in Civ 6 were working against me, so I've added options to disable or simplify them.

Civ 6 isn't a bad game, but compared to previous Civ games it requires the player to make many more decisions which can lead to decision fatigue, and many times these decisions aren't very interesting.

What's worse, unlike previous Civ games, these systems are not optional as the player is given no way in-game to disable the systems she finds undesirable.

Even Firaxis realised some of these systems were an issue:

> Beach claims players "hated" the pacing of Civilization VI. It's an admission that resulted in Firaxis conducting individual postmortems of each system to learn when they pushed "important, strategic decisions you don't have to make more than every five to 10 minutes," and when they demanded players make "tiresome 'click here, click here, click, here' decisions that just aren't very interesting."

([Firaxis' big swing with Civilization VII? Convincing players to actually finish their games](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/design/firaxis-big-swing-with-civilization-vii-convincing-players-to-actually-finish-their-games))

Another take:

> ... the district system reduces much of your planning time—usually an imperial glance across a map, or a fun little act of construction—to agonising over numbers. I just want to build something, man, I don’t want to be the Math Lady meme every time I need a new harbour.

> That’s just one example, but this board gamey emphasis on combos and sums, on systems layered over ideas layered over systems, is everywhere. There is–and I can’t believe I’m saying this about a strategy game–just too much going on. The World Congress is an absolute chore to click through, reducing what could have been a chance for delicate global communication to a series of simple +/- decisions. The entire civics concept, so great on paper, is another I just click straight through. Same for era scores, and diplomacy, and and gah!

[Sid Meier’s Civilization VI: The Kotaku Re-Review](https://kotaku.com/civilization-civ-vi-6-review-2023-retrospective-pc-sid-1850242218)

## Game systems

#### Policy cards

Policy cards are a perfect example of what's wrong with Civ 6. They give the players a way to customise their civilisation and choose the specific benefits they want. At the beginning of the game they're engaging and interesting, and they feel great.

But as the game goes on, the player will end up with dozens of policy cards. Each time the player gets a new one they have to not only examine the new policy cards but compare it to all other policy cards accrued in the game to decide whether it's worth switching. As the game progresses, this becomes more and more fatiguing.

Policy cards are a great idea with a poor implementation, which seems to be applicable to many of the new systems in Civ 6.

#### Religious combat

When Civ 5 implemented one-unit-per-tile, this drastically changed the combat in the game from smashing large stacks of units against each other to a more wargame-like tactical combat system. While this can overstay its welcome, most would agree it's a better system.

With Civ 6 they decided to extend this to religion and they implemented religious combat. On paper this sounds like a fun idea, but having a second parallel combat system in the game quickly gets tedious.

Rather than being added as a major game system, this would have been better as a one-off gimmick added to a scenario.

#### Great people

Great people are a perfect example of how Civ 6 adds more decisions. In previous Civ games, great people automatically appear as soon as a player earns them.

In Civ 6, first the player is prompted to click a button to _Claim Great Person_, which opens the Great People dialogue. Then the player has to find which great people they are eligible for, and then decide whether to recruit them or reject them and wait for another. The player can also purchase great people with gold or faith.

Surely some find this deeper engagement with great people interesting, but personally it feels tedious. It results in more clicks and more decisions in a game which (compared to previous Civs) has already too many.

#### Builders

Builders feel like just one more decision point, but frequently not an interesting one. Often a tile only has one improvement that can be placed, which means there isn't even a decision to be made, just work to do.

Combined with the decision of when to build a builder, how many to build, which improvements to place, it all contributes to decision fatigue.

Builders also require the players to focus on and care about individual map tiles. Many enjoy this form of micromanagement but it doesn't seem to fit well thematically with a game that's ostensibly about building an empire.

#### Districts

Districts are a great idea. Some have compared Civ 6 to a city builder. Districts are interesting and provide the player a way to specialise cities. However they also contribute to decision fatigue; these are just some of the considerations players are faced with when placing a district:

- Which district should be built in this city?
- What districts are in other cities?
- Where should the district be placed?
- Would the district provide more benefits from the feature or improvement that gets removed when it's placed?

#### Amenities

The amenities system seems like like just the latest iteration of previous systems like corruption, health, happiness. Like much in Civ 6, it's fine. But it doesn't feel like it adds much to the game.

#### Civics

Putting civics into a separate tree is a very interesting idea. But like many of the other systems, it's just one more decision to make.

## Too much of everything

In addition to problems with particular systems, each successive Civ game (with the exception of [CivRev](./civrev-mode.md)) keeps adding **more**. More buildings, improvements, recourses, techs, units, wonders, and on and on. At some point it's just too much, contributing yet again to decision fatigue.

However, that not all that's been added feels that way. City states, civilisations, scenarios, and game modes are all features that the player can choose to engage with (or not).
